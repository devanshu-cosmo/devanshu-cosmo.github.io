## Background

Primordial Black Holes (PBHs) are one of the most studied topics in cosmology, particularly in the past decade, and there are compelling reasons for this. But first, what exactly are PBHs, and how are they different from the regular black holes (BHs)?

In the common literature, stellar BHs (also called astrophysical BHs or ABHs) are formed when massive stars reach the end of their life and run out of thermonuclear fuel. The star cannot sustain its inward gravitational pull, which eventually leads to its gravitational collapse, forming an ABH.

PBHs, as their name suggests, are primordial or, literally, **as old as time itself**. PBHs could have formed in the **very early Universe**, just fractions of a second after the Big Bang.

Today, PBHs are known to have potential answers for:

- the nature of dark matter
- the origin of supermassive black holes
- the contribution to the stochastic gravitational wave background  

and many more! The research on PBHs has been going on for more than five decades, since Y. Zeldovich's seminal paper in '67. However, the interest in PBHs exploded when the LIGO-VIRGO collaborations reported a signal from two merging black holes in 2015.

## Production of PBHs

The most popular formation mechanism for PBHs has to be from the gravitational collapse of inflationary fluctuations. 

During inflation (a brief period of rapid expansion immediately after the Big Bang), tiny quantum fluctuations are stretched to cosmological scales. If some of these fluctuations are **large enough**, they can later collapse under gravity when they re-enter the horizon. 

The intuition for PBH formation is straightforward, but its mathematical counterpart requires a sophisticated workflow. During my master's years, I worked on precisely the same workflow. I studied how PBHs can be generated in **single-field inflation models**.

The primary motivation was to engineer a phase where the power spectrum grows significantly on small scales (called an **ultra slow-roll** phase), while remaining consistent with CMB observations on large scales.

The execution was done through a series of steps, including:

- solving inflationary dynamics through numerically solving complex ODEs
- Fine-tuning and sensitivity analysis for more than 5 model parameters and collecting multiple sets of optimal combinations
- Computing the **scalar power spectrum** using ODE solvers throughout the span of inflation
- Estimating the statistical abundance of PBHs resulting from the enhancement in the power spectrum
- Analyzing the datasets from several observations to build constraints on PBH abundance
