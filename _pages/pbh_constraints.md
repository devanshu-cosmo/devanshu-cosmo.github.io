## Background

You will find Supermassive black holes (SMBH) at the centre of almost all the galaxies in the universe. They play a key role in galaxy formation and evolution. 

A question one could ask is: What is the origin of such SMBHs? Turns out that there are multiple possible candidates (**but no smoking gun proof, so far!**):

- Direct gravitational collapse of large mass gas clouds
- Merging stellar mass BHs
- Gravitational collapse of Population III stars
- **Primordial Black Holes (PBHs)**

Here, we shall focus on PBHs as the **forefathers** of the SMBHs. It makes perfect sense to start with heavy PBHs in the early universe. They would have all the time in the world (literally!) to accumulate matter and evolve into the giant SMBHs today. However, there is a catch.

**What are PBHs?**: Multiple mechanisms in the standard model of cosmology could have led to the formation of BHs in the very early universe, much before the non-relativistic matter came into existence. These are known as PBHs. In a popular formation mechanism, PBHs could be generated by the large quantum fluctuations from cosmic inflation.

**The Catch**: If we assume that PBHs were produced from inflationary density fluctuations, we find that the same fluctuations also leave observable imprints in the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB), in the form of spectral distortions.

During my PhD, I tried to answer the questions:

> Can PBHs form without violating CMB constraints? What role does primordial non-Gaussianity play in this scenario?

---

## The Need for Non-Gaussianity

Primordial perturbations undergo **Silk Damping**, where they essentially dissipate their energy to the photon-baryon fluid, disrupting the blackbody spectrum of the photons, which could be observed as spectral distortions in the Blackbody spectrum of the CMB today. 

PBHs form from rare, large fluctuations in the primordial density field, which are inherently random. Hence, the fluctuations concerning PBHs reside in the tail of the probability distribution (PDF). Naturally, even a little positively non-Gaussian tail in the PDF would boost the population of PBHs, meaning that there would be a higher number of PBHs to seed the SMBHs. 

On the other hand, for Gaussian initial conditions, there are tight constraints on massive PBHs (or SM-PBHs) that could explain SMBHs, all thanks to the non-detection of spectral distortions. Forming PBHs above roughly $10^4 M_\odot$ is strongly constrained in the Gaussian case.

---

### 1. Quadratic Non-Gaussianity


Our team performed the first consistent calculation of spectral distortion constraints in the presence of non-Gaussianity. Since there are several ways (or shapes) in which non-Gaussianity can exist in a statistical dataset, one needs to assume a specific template of non-Gaussianity before computing the statistical correlators of the stochastic variable. We worked with the most common **quadratic non-Gaussianity**.

We use our earlier work where we have made the first calculation of spectral distortions subject to non-Gaussianity. We compute the statistical abundance of PBHs for a non-Gaussian PDF and connect the spectral distortion constraints to the PBH constraints.

The intersection of these two constraints provides an upper limit on the mass and population (measured normalized to dark matter fraction, $f_{\rm PBH}$) of PBHs. The figure below portrays our results.

![Quadratic non-Gaussian Constraints](/assets/images/plotG2.jpg)

*The $$\mu$$-distortion and PBH constraints on the power spectrum amplitude of primordial perturbations for a quadratic non-Gaussianity. The intersection sets the maximum allowed PBH mass.*

When we compared the results with the standard Gaussian results, we were surprised because:

- Even **extremely large non-Gaussianity** does not significantly relax the constraints  
- The allowed PBH mass scale barely changes

Our results came as a disappointment to the cosmology community, as a large number of researchers were hoping that non-Gaussianity could let PBHs be a viable explanation to the SMBHs. Recent observations, such as those from JWST, have revealed surprisingly massive black holes at high redshift, further motivating primordial scenarios. Given the JWST discoveries, people were looking at PBHs with the rays of hope. Nonetheless, our results helped the community close a chapter with the following implication:

> Primordial non-Gaussianity only weakly relaxes the constraints on PBHs. One requires very strong non-Gaussianity to generate a significant amount of primordially seeded SMBHs.

---

### 2. Cubic Non-Gaussianity

To push this further, our team extended the calculation to **cubic non-Gaussianity**, which is stronger than the quadratic non-Gaussianity. Carrying out the mathematical derivations was not a cakewalk, since the process involved:

- Computing **2-loop contributions** to the power spectrum  
- Evaluating convolution integrals in Fourier space  
- Deriving analytic expressions for highly non-trivial integrals  

Despite this added complexity, we found out that

- Even in the limit of **extreme cubic non-Gaussianity**, the spectral distortion constraints remain **remarkably similar** to the Gaussian case
- Although the maximum allowed SM-PBH mass relaxes by three orders of magnitude, the PBH abundance is still tightly bound.
- We find that for quartic and quintic non-Gaussianities, at least a small number of PBHs can account for the present-day SMBHs.

![Cubic non-Gaussian Constraints](/assets/images/plotG3-G.jpg)

*The $$\mu$$-distortion and PBH constraints on the power spectrum amplitude of primordial perturbations for a cubic non-Gaussianity. The intersection sets the maximum allowed PBH mass.*

If I were to summarize all our results and their implications for cosmology, I would say:

> Owing to strong bounds on the amplitude of CMB spectral distortions sourced by primordial perturbations, primordial Non-Gaussianity does not easily let PBH seed the SMBHs. Extreme non-Gaussianities might allow a small fraction of SMBHs of primordial origin, but they lack a physical backing.

## The Technicals

These projects required combining theory, statistics, and computation at a fairly deep level.

Some of the key elements were:

- Modeling **non-Gaussian random fields** beyond the perturbative regime  
- Computing **higher-order correlation functions** and loop corrections  
- Solving **multi-dimensional convolution integrals** in Fourier space    
- Extending the **CLASS cosmology code** to include non-Gaussian effects  
- Mapping theoretical predictions to **observable CMB constraints**
